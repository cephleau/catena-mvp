### Post 1 — Process Improvement (Boeing/Engineering Lens)

**The hidden cost of undocumented tribal knowledge**

The most expensive process in your company isn't the broken one. It's the one that only works because one specific person is in the room.

I saw this constantly at Boeing. Highly complex operations, tight tolerances, serious stakes — and somewhere buried in there, a critical handoff that existed only in someone's head. No SOP. No checklist. No backup.

When that person goes on leave, gets promoted, or leaves? The process doesn't fail dramatically. It degrades slowly. Errors creep in. Rework quietly doubles. And nobody connects the dots because the work *technically* still gets done.

The fix isn't complicated — it's just uncomfortable. It means sitting down with your best people and making them document the thing they've been doing for years without thinking about it. It means treating institutional knowledge as a liability when it isn't captured.

Most companies skip this because it feels low-priority until it isn't.

The real question: how many of your processes are one resignation away from chaos?

### Post 2 — Data Governance

**Data governance isn't a tech problem. It's a people problem dressed in tech clothes.**

Most companies approach data governance by buying software.

They invest in data catalogs, lineage tools, quality dashboards — and then wonder why nothing changes six months later. The data is still inconsistent. Departments still argue about whose numbers are right. The reports still don't match.

Here's what's actually happening: governance isn't failing because of missing tooling. It's failing because nobody agreed on the basics *before* the tools went in.

Who owns this data? What does "complete" mean for this field? Which system is the source of truth when two systems disagree? These aren't technical questions — they're organizational ones. And most teams skip them because they're politically uncomfortable.

I've worked in environments where answering "what's our defect rate?" could produce three different numbers from three different teams — all of them technically correct, none of them useful.

Real data governance starts with a conversation, not a software license. Get aligned on definitions, ownership, and accountability first. Then automate.

What's the most contested data point at your company right now?

### Post 3 — Lean Six Sigma + Modern Ops

**Lean wasn't built for factories. It was built for waste — and waste is everywhere.**

People hear "Lean Six Sigma" and picture assembly lines. Stopwatches. Clipboards. Manufacturing floor efficiency.

That framing has cost a lot of companies a lot of money.

The core insight behind lean — eliminate anything that consumes resources without creating value — applies just as hard to a SaaS onboarding flow, a hiring process, or a weekly executive report that nobody reads but everyone produces.

I've run DMAIC cycles on processes that had nothing to do with manufacturing. Customer escalation workflows. Budget approval chains. Internal data request queues. The tools translate cleanly. The discipline translates better.

What usually doesn't translate is the cultural part: the willingness to look at a process you've run for years and ask "what would this look like if we built it from scratch today?" That question is uncomfortable. It implies that the current way might not be the best way.

The companies that ask it anyway are usually the ones that compound their improvements instead of one-and-done-ing them.

Lean isn't a manufacturing methodology. It's a mindset about waste.

Where's the most obvious waste in your operation right now — the thing everyone sees but nobody's touched?
